Breaking into the K-pop market is as difficult as threading a needle, according to a new study released on August 4. Professor Kim Jeong-seop of Sungshin Women's University conducted a comprehensive analysis of all 1,182 idol groups that debuted over the 30 years following H.O.T.'s debut in 1996, finding that only 1.61% — just 19 groups — achieved million-seller status with a single album.

Professor Kim described the research as 'the first case of a precise investigation into all 1,182 groups that debuted over 30 years from H.O.T. in 1996 through the end of 2025, analyzed using statistical analysis, cohort analysis, and expert in-depth interview techniques.' He added that the study 'empirically identified the internal competitive structure and hit mechanisms of the K-pop idol industry, which has achieved global success.'

The study found that an average of 39.4 groups debuted each year — approximately 19.8 boy groups and 19.6 girl groups — but the market is characterized by high early dropout rates alongside a winner-takes-all, high-risk, high-competition structure. The average survival period for all idol groups was 4.12 years, which equals 58.9% of the standard exclusive contract period of 7 years. Only 55.03% of groups survived past the three-year mark, meaning roughly 45% of all groups exit the market within three years.

Professor Kim noted that 'a significant number of groups end their activities or effectively disband before completing their first exclusive contract period, due to the industry's unique structure in which market performance shown during the first one to three years after debut determines whether agencies continue investing from the fourth year onward.' He also observed that the three-year survival rate for idol groups was higher than the 41.5% rate for general small and medium-sized businesses, but lower than the 68.1% rate for government-supported startups.

Boy groups had an average survival period of 5.11 years compared to 3.13 years for girl groups — a gap of approximately 1.98 years. This difference was attributed to contrasting revenue models: boy groups benefit from a highly loyal, female fandom-driven consumer base, while girl groups rely more on general public appeal and income from events and digital music sales. Among all groups, only 42 teams (3.55%) sold over 300,000 copies of a single album — considered a break-even level of success — and only 7 groups (0.59%) surpassed 10 million cumulative album sales, establishing a solid global IP brand: BTS, SEVENTEEN, Stray Kids, EXO, TWICE, NCT, and TXT.

Professor Kim interpreted BTS's success as not 'a one-off exception,' but as evidence that 'a post-BTS era has formed in which a small number of subsequent globally popular groups have also emerged.' He proposed several policy priorities for the sustainable development of the K-pop industry, including introducing long-term idol development systems, creating pathways for re-challenges after failure, providing shared infrastructure support for small and mid-sized agencies, curbing indiscriminate debuts through stronger pre-verification, and balancing global expansion with domestic sustainability. The research paper was published on July 30 in Volume 20, Issue 4 of the Journal of the Korea Entertainment Industry Association, under the title 'Survival and Hit Structure of the K-pop Idol Music Industry: A Comprehensive Analysis of 1,182 Groups That Debuted Over 30 Years (1996–2025) Since H.O.T.'
