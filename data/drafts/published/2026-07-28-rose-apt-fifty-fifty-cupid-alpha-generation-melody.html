What do BLACKPINK Rosé and Bruno Mars's hit 'APT.', FIFTY FIFTY's 'Cupid', Ava Max's 'So Am I', and Clairo's 'Sofia' all have in common? On July 27 (local time), The Washington Post introduced a musical similarity discovered by a Danish musicologist across songs ranging from K-pop to American pop, and music from Egypt, India, Spain, Italy, and other countries around the world.

Danish musicologist Carl Martin spent time browsing TikTok, Spotify, and Reddit, listening to hundreds of songs from across the globe before narrowing them down to 72 tracks that sounded distinctly similar. He found that these songs are built around four central notes and carry an emotional quality that is simultaneously hopeful and melancholic.

Martin named this pattern the 'Alpha Generation Melody' because the characteristic appeared most prominently in songs released after the 2010s. He then compiled these tracks into a 14-minute YouTube video.

The video opens with Rosé and Bruno Mars's 'APT.' and within just one minute weaves together snippets of nine songs: 'So Am I', 'Reckless', 'Forever', 'One of Your Girls', 'Sofia', 'Ode to the Mets', 'Cupid', and 'Lie to Me'. It also places alongside them songs with slight variations, such as Lady Gaga's 'Bad Romance' and One Direction's 'Night Changes'. YouTube users expressed amazement at how smoothly the different songs flowed together as if they were one, and the video went viral online, racking up 3.4 million views within two months of being posted.

Nate Sloan, a musicologist at the University of Southern California (USC), called it 'quite shocking to hear all these melodies back to back' in the early part of the video. He added that the 'Alpha Generation Melody' is characterized by a cyclical structure, saying the songs 'go round and round, like a carousel — much like the infinite-scroll content younger generations have grown up consuming.'

However, experts viewed this similarity as a trend rather than plagiarism. Martin analyzed it not as imitation, but as many songwriters independently arriving at the same conclusion. Sloan also referenced how, from Handel to Rachmaninoff, more than 150 composers over three centuries were influenced by the Iberian dance form 'La Folía' to create similar music, explaining: 'People say they want to hear something new, but often they end up wanting to hear something familiar.'
